 Chapter 2: Justice Between Theory and Practice

  

The pursuit of justice represents one of humanity's most enduring challenges. As Dostoevsky illustrates in *Crime and Punishment*, individuals must "persistently strive towards doing the right thing for the right reason" (Larkins, 1996, p. 605). This seemingly straightforward principle becomes profoundly complex when translated from theory into institutional practice, creating tensions that challenge both scholars and state actors alike (Negretto & Sánchez-Talanquer, 2024).

This transition from abstract moral principles to concrete legal frameworks reveals inherent contradictions that exist across diverse legal traditions (Fuller, 1964). When moral ideals meet institutional realities, spaces emerge where injustice can flourish—sometimes despite, or even because of, adherence to formal legal structures.

Much like a legal system comprises both legislation and enforcement, the legitimacy of justice requires two complementary elements: proper intention (*mens rea*) and appropriate action (*actus reus*) (Weingast, 1997). Just as both elements must be present for legal culpability, effective justice demands both properly formulated laws and ethical implementation through fair procedures and moral discernment (Dworkin, 1986).

  The challenge lies in bridging this gap. Simply following established rules without moral reflection risks moral abdication. History has repeatedly shown how "following orders" can become a hallmark of authoritarian regimes. This defense, famously rejected at the Nuremberg Trials, demonstrates how legal compliance divorced from ethical reasoning can enable systemic oppression rather than justice (Arendt, 1963). These trials established that legal orders themselves can be illegitimate when they violate fundamental human dignity, suggesting a higher moral law transcending positive legal enactments (Taylor, 1992).

  Conversely, breaking established law to pursue extrajudicial "justice" risks creating new forms of injustice through arbitrary enforcement and lack of procedural safeguards (Nozick, 1974). This presents a fundamental dilemma for justice practitioners who must reconcile their legal responsibilities with personal moral codes. The tension between legal positivism and natural law theory remains unresolved in both theoretical discourse and practical jurisprudence, suggesting that justice requires ongoing negotiation between competing normative frameworks rather than rigid adherence to either extreme (Hart, 1961).

Despite its objective existence in rational human thought, justice becomes subjective in practice due to inherent differences in individual and cultural perspectives. This subjectivity manifests across multiple governance levels—from international variations to regional differences within nations—revealing how contextual our conceptions of justice truly are (Rawls, 1971). Different societies conceptualize justice differently, reflecting profound philosophical and cultural disagreements about human flourishing and social order (Walzer, 1983).
In this chapter, we examine how these tensions between universal principles and local implementation shape transitional justice efforts in contexts where democratic values face significant challenges.

   Individual versus Collective: The Fundamental Tension in Governance
Governments ostensibly exist to serve their citizens—a theoretical principle necessary for maintaining social order. Throughout human history, political, philosophical, cultural, and legal debates have centered on defining what truly constitutes "the benefit of the people."

Modern governance has evolved primarily around two contrasting approaches: empowering individuals or strengthening collective society. Liberal democracy firmly embraces the former philosophy, championing individual rights, liberties, and opportunities. This bottom-up approach assumes that when individuals flourish, communities naturally prosper through the aggregation of individual achievements and contributions.

 In contrast, undemocratic regimes typically employ a top-down approach where power concentrates in identifiable individuals or institutions rather than being distributed. While democratic societies with market economies often feature diffuse power structures—where government officials, labor unions, corporations, and advocacy groups all influence outcomes—authoritarian systems maintain clearly defined power hierarchies that control societal change.

 Vladimir Putin's early presidency provides a compelling case study in how democratic structures can be methodically dismantled. His systematic restriction of judicial independence represents what Epstein, Knight, and Shvetsova (2001) identified as a deliberate transformation of constitutional protections into mere procedural formalities rather than substantive checks on power. Rather than overtly attacking democratic institutions, his approach involved subtle recalibration of existing structures to consolidate authority without triggering immediate alarm.

This pattern of institutional erosion has emerged across various contexts with remarkable consistency. The sophisticated strategies employed in Bangladesh, characterized by Bajpai and Kureshi (2022, p. 595) as "democratic authoritarianism," demonstrate how electoral processes themselves can be manipulated to provide a veneer of legitimacy for authoritarian control. In these systems, the instruments of modernization become powerful tools for limiting democratic participation rather than expanding it.

 The international community has struggled to respond effectively to these evolved forms of authoritarianism. Traditional democracy promotion approaches, as Kurze and Lamont (2022) observe, often prove inadequate against regimes that maintain democratic appearances while systematically undermining democratic substance. This presents a fundamental challenge: how can genuine democratic values be preserved when the very mechanisms designed to protect them become instruments of their erosion?

  The Paradox of Popular Participation: Institutional Erosion in Quasi-Democratic Systems

The systematic dismantling of democratic institutions occurs not through dramatic coups but through methodical legal manipulation. Putin's Russia exemplifies this approach, where Epstein, Knight, and Shvetsova (2001) documented how judicial independence was undermined through subtle recalibration that transformed constitutional protections into procedural formalities rather than substantive checks on power. The 2001 Law on Political Parties illustrates how technical regulations—requiring branches in 45 regions with minimum membership of 10,000—effectively eliminated opposition through administrative means rather than overt repression.

This pattern has manifested globally with remarkable consistency. Bangladesh's 2018 elections represent a particularly instructive case study, where Riaz and Parvez (2021) identified a coordinated strategy of electoral control through three mechanisms: capturing civilian administration to pressure opposition, deploying law enforcement to intimidate voters, and co-opting electoral commissions to validate questionable results. The statistical improbability of 96% ruling party support should have triggered international concern but instead demonstrated how democratic appearances can mask authoritarian realities.

The weaponization of modernization tools for political control follows predictable patterns across hybrid regimes. Bangladesh's Digital Security Act demonstrates how security legislation transforms into systematic oppression through deliberately vague language. As Miaji and Islam (2023) analyze, provisions regarding "anti-state activities" and "deterioration of law and order" create expansive linguistic vessels that authorities can fill with whatever content they find inconvenient. Rahman and Rashid's research (2020) shows these ambiguous standards effectively silence journalists without requiring mass arrests, inducing widespread self-censorship.

Bangladesh represents what Eisenstadt, Levan, and Maboudi (2017) might term an experimental space for authoritarian innovation—a laboratory where democratic forms persist while democratic substance is methodically extracted. The country exhibits what Haque (2018) identifies as formal separation of powers alongside systematic executive dominance. Constitutional changes, particularly the Fifteenth Amendment eliminating the caretaker government system, maintain democratic appearances while concentrating power behind institutional facades.

 The international community's response has proven inadequate against these evolved governance forms. Kurze and Lamont (2022) demonstrate how traditional democracy promotion methods fail against authoritarian systems that maintain sufficient democratic appearances to avoid diplomatic consequences. Bermeo (2016) observes that democratic backsliding has evolved precisely to exploit international monitoring blind spots, replacing easily condemned coups with gradually implemented, legally cloaked approaches that achieve similar outcomes without triggering intervention.

 This evolution from overt to covert authoritarianism presents a fundamental challenge: how can democratic principles be preserved when the very institutions designed to protect them become instruments of their erosion?

JusticeCraft as a Framework for Democratic Renewal

The sophisticated legal barriers to democracy in modern authoritarian systems demand innovative approaches that transcend traditional reform paradigms. These barriers have evolved beyond simple repressive measures into complex legal architectures that maintain democratic appearances while systematically undermining democratic substance. Such evolution represents what Bermeo (2016, p. 10) terms "executive aggrandizement"—where elected executives gradually erode checks on their authority through seemingly legitimate legal channels rather than through overt coups.

The JusticeCraft framework, developed by Balasco and colleagues (2024), offers a promising conceptual and practical approach for navigating these challenges. Unlike conventional transitional justice frameworks that typically prescribe linear, institution-focused interventions, JusticeCraft represents a substantive reconceptualization of how justice operates in transitionally complex environments. This framework emerged as a direct response to what the authors diplomatically termed "the inadequacy of linear, top-down approaches to transitional justice"—an academic euphemism for the frequent failures of conventional methods when confronted with the adaptive mechanisms of modern authoritarian systems.

The theoretical foundations of JusticeCraft draw upon both established and emerging scholarly traditions. From established literature, it incorporates Teitel's (2000, p. 223) understanding of transitional justice as inherently contextual and contingent, requiring adaptation to local conditions rather than rigid application of universal templates. From emerging scholarship, JusticeCraft incorporates Sharp's (2018, p. 156) critique of the "liberal peace" paradigm and its tendency to impose Western institutional models without sufficient attention to local power dynamics.

The framework also builds upon O'Donnell and Schmitter's (1986, p. 38) classic insights regarding the negotiated nature of democratic transitions, where change occurs through complex bargaining processes rather than through revolutionary ruptures. As Kapstein and Converse (2008, p. 57) demonstrate in their analysis of 123 democratic transitions, the sustainability of democratic reforms depends significantly on institutional designs that distribute power broadly rather than concentrating it in executive branches. This insight becomes particularly relevant for Bangladesh, where constitutional amendments have progressively centralized executive authority while maintaining democratic appearances (Riaz, 2020b, p. 612).

 Central to this theoretical foundation is Dahl's (1971, p. 3) conception of polyarchy—the institutional arrangements that facilitate meaningful political competition and inclusive participation. The JusticeCraft framework expands on this conception by emphasizing how democratic competition requires not merely formal procedures but substantive protections for oppositional voices. This expansion aligns with Waldner and Lust's (2018, p. 95) observation that contemporary democratic breakdown typically occurs through incremental institutional modifications rather than through dramatic regime changes—a pattern clearly evident in Bangladesh's democratic regression.

  

What distinguishes JusticeCraft from its theoretical predecessors is its emphasis on the materiality of justice—how abstract principles become concrete practices and institutions. In contexts like Bangladesh, this means moving beyond theoretical calls for democratic reform to address specific legal provisions such as Article 70 of the Constitution (which restricts parliamentary autonomy), the ambiguous provisions of the Digital Security Act, and preventive detention laws facilitating political repression (Hoque, 1995, p. 187). By focusing on these tangible manifestations of injustice, reform efforts can precisely target leverage points within the legal architecture.

  

The framework incorporates Levitsky and Way's (2010, p. 72) insights regarding competitive authoritarianism—regimes that maintain democratic forms while systematically violating democratic substance. Their distinction between the "organizational power" and "coercive capacity" of authoritarian systems helps explain why Bangladesh's democratic regression has proven particularly resilient. As Maitrot and Jackman (2020, p. 27) observe, Bangladesh has developed sophisticated mechanisms for maintaining electoral facades while systematically eliminating meaningful competition—including through selective law enforcement, administrative constraints on opposition, and strategic deployment of ambiguous legal provisions.

  

The JusticeCraft framework's emphasis on knowledge production holds particular significance in contexts where legal manipulation has been accompanied by ideational capture—control over how justice and democracy are conceptualized and discussed. This requires establishing alternative knowledge networks capable of articulating principled critiques of legal manipulation and developing contextually appropriate reform proposals. As Slater (2013, p. 734) demonstrates in his comparative analysis of authoritarian durability, regimes that successfully monopolize definitional power—controlling how concepts like "democracy" and "stability" are understood—can maintain authoritarian practices behind democratic facades for extended periods.

  

Bangladesh's manipulation of democratic terminology, particularly regarding judicial independence and electoral integrity, exemplifies this pattern (Riaz & Parvez, 2021, p. 808).

The framework also confronts what Carothers (2002, p. 15) identified as the "sequence fallacy"—the mistaken belief that certain institutional developments must necessarily precede others in democratic transitions. Instead, JusticeCraft advocates for what might be termed "democratic simultaneity"—the parallel development of multiple democratic capabilities through networked reform efforts rather than through rigid institutional sequencing. This theoretical reorientation recognizes that in contexts where authoritarian systems have weaponized legal structures, democratic progress requires coordinated pressure across multiple domains rather than concentrated focus on individual institutions.

  

This conceptual innovation aligns with Acemoglu and Robinson's (2019, p. 387) distinction between "cage of norms" and "paper leviathan" states—with Bangladesh increasingly resembling the latter, where formal democratic institutions exist but systematic informal violations render them ineffective. The JusticeCraft framework addresses this challenge by emphasizing how democratic renewal requires simultaneous attention to formal rules and informal practices, recognizing that authoritarian systems maintain power through the strategic manipulation of the gap between democratic appearance and authoritarian reality (Shams, 2024, p. 38).

  

 Dimensions of JusticeCraft: Resistance, Reclamation, and Resilience in Practice

  

Central to the JusticeCraft framework are three interconnected dimensions—resistance, reclamation, and resilience—deliberately integrated rather than sequentially arranged, reflecting the messy reality of democratic struggles rather than the neat theoretical progressions that rarely survive contact with political complexity.

  

The resistance dimension acknowledges the necessity of challenging unjust legal structures, not as acts of lawlessness but as demonstrations of commitment to broader principles of justice. In Bangladesh, this has manifested through strategic litigation against unconstitutional provisions, exemplified by the January 2020 constitutional challenge to Sections 25 and 31 of the Digital Security Act filed by nine petitioners including journalists, professors, and legal practitioners (Prothom Alo, 2023). When the High Court issued a rule on February 24, 2020, questioning why these provisions shouldn't be declared contradictory to constitutional protections, it created a formal space for contesting authoritarian legality, though the case was later removed from the cause list without resolution—itself a demonstration of how legal resistance often encounters procedural obstructions.

  

Documentation efforts have been crucial in building resistance through knowledge creation. The Centre for Governance Studies meticulously tracked 890 cases from January 2020 to February 2022, documenting patterns that revealed the law's disproportionate targeting of journalists and critics (Centre for Governance Studies, 2022). Their research demonstrated that among those accused whose professions were identified, more than a quarter were journalists—207 media professionals in a 26-month period. This systematic documentation transforms individual cases into recognizable patterns of repression, creating an evidence base that challenges the government's narrative of legitimate security enforcement.

  

The resistance dimension aligns with what O'Brien and Li (2006, p. 2) termed "rightful resistance"—challenges to authority that employ the rhetoric and commitments of powerful elites to expose contradictions between official claims and actual practices. In Bangladesh's context, this involves highlighting discrepancies between constitutional guarantees of free expression and the systematic application of digital security laws to silence critics. This approach draws upon Ekiert and Kubik's (1999, p. 188) observation that effective resistance often operates through "institutional interstices"—exploiting contradictions within authoritarian legal systems rather than confronting them directly.

  

The contested provisions clearly demonstrate why such resistance is necessary. Section 25 of the DSA penalizes publishing "offensive, false or threatening" information with imprisonment up to three years and substantial fines, while Section 31 more severely punishes content creating "enmity, hatred or hostility among different classes or communities" with up to seven years imprisonment (Prothom Alo, 2023). These provisions employ deliberately ambiguous language that transforms expression itself into a potentially criminal act. Legal scholars have extensively analyzed how these provisions create "vague and overly broad" restrictions that produce "a chilling effect on expressions on matters of public concern" (Sabera, 2022, p. 45).

  

This legislative ambiguity represents what Schedler (2013, p. 58) identifies as "institutional bias"—where seemingly neutral rules systematically disadvantage opposition forces through selective or unpredictable enforcement. Rahman and Rashid's (2020, p. 62) analysis of Digital Security Act enforcement demonstrates how this ambiguity facilitates "self-censorship cascades" where journalists internalize restrictions to avoid potential prosecution, effectively extending the reach of formal restrictions through informal mechanisms of anticipatory compliance.

  

The reclamation dimension of JusticeCraft involves restoring democratic principles and practices that have been obscured or corrupted by authoritarian legal maneuvering. Unlike traditional approaches focused simply on restoring institutions to previous states, reclamation acknowledges that some entities may require fundamental reimagining rather than mere rehabilitation. For Bangladesh, reclamation encompasses recovering constitutional values that predated authoritarian amendments, reestablishing judicial independence as a substantive principle rather than a procedural formality, and revitalizing concepts of political accountability that have been systematically undermined.

  

The resilience dimension of JusticeCraft holds particular relevance for Bangladesh's context. Building democratic resilience requires creating institutional redundancies and social networks capable of withstanding legal attacks on democratic space. This crucial lesson emerges from numerous failed democratic transitions where progress collapsed when key institutions were compromised. Resilience encompasses developing alternative media platforms capable of functioning despite digital restrictions, establishing professional solidarity networks among lawyers and judges, and creating cross-border connections with democratic movements facing similar challenges.

  

JusticeCraft's critical insight is that these three processes—resistance, reclamation, and resilience—must function simultaneously rather than sequentially. Resistance without reclamation risks descending into nihilism, reclamation without resistance may become merely nostalgic, and neither can succeed without resilience against inevitable setbacks. By integrating these dimensions, the framework provides a comprehensive approach to addressing Bangladesh's legal barriers to democracy.

  

 Operationalizing JusticeCraft: Strategies for Sustainable Democratic Transition

  

Moving from theoretical framework to practical implementation requires developing concrete strategies that operationalize the JusticeCraft approach. These implementation strategies must address the specific legal barriers to democracy in Bangladesh while building long-term capacity for democratic renewal. Based on comparative experiences from countries that have successfully navigated similar challenges, several promising approaches emerge.

  

Cross-sectoral coalitions represent a crucial strategy, uniting legal professionals, students, journalists, and other civil society actors around specific legal reforms. This approach builds on the successful coalition-building that ultimately broke Malaysia's political impasse in 2018, creating a sufficiently broad reform constituency to overcome entrenched interests. These coalitions function most effectively when organized around concrete objectives rather than abstract principles, focusing reform energies on specific legal provisions with demonstrable antidemocratic effects.

  

The development of "democracy resilience hubs" provides another promising approach, creating spaces where abuses can be documented, resistance strategies coordinated, and reform proposals developed even under restrictive conditions. These hubs, which may function as physical spaces, virtual networks, or hybrid structures, serve as repositories of democratic knowledge and practice when formal institutions become compromised. The establishment of such hubs in Poland and Hungary has demonstrated their effectiveness in preserving democratic capacity during periods of authoritarian consolidation.

  

Measuring progress within the JusticeCraft framework requires moving beyond traditional democratic indicators that focus primarily on electoral processes. Meaningful metrics must capture both procedural and substantive dimensions of democratic development, assessing not merely whether elections occur but whether they enable genuine political competition. The V-Dem Institute's (2023) multidimensional democracy indices offer promising approaches for tracking democratic progress across multiple dimensions, including judicial independence, media freedom, and civil society autonomy.

  

Digital documentation initiatives have proven particularly effective in contexts where traditional monitoring mechanisms have been compromised. The systematic collection of digital evidence of rights violations, legislative overreach, and judicial manipulation creates both an evidentiary base for future accountability and a contemporary challenge to authoritarian narratives. These documentation efforts transform isolated incidents into recognizable patterns of repression, making visible the systematic nature of democratic erosion that might otherwise remain obscured by case-by-case analysis.

  

Strategic litigation, even in compromised judicial systems, serves multiple functions within the JusticeCraft framework. Beyond their potential to secure specific legal victories, strategic cases create formal spaces for articulating democratic principles, documenting antidemocratic practices, and building professional solidarity among legal practitioners. The January-February 2020 constitutional challenge to the Digital Security Act in Bangladesh exemplifies this approach, creating formal space for contesting authoritarian legality even if immediate victories remain elusive.

  

International engagement strategies represent another crucial component of operationalizing JusticeCraft, though these must be carefully calibrated to avoid undermining local ownership of democratic struggles. Rather than imposing external models, effective international engagement focuses on creating protective space for domestic democratic actors, providing technical assistance based on locally determined priorities, and maintaining consistent attention to democratic principles rather than shifting with geopolitical winds. The inconsistent international response to Bangladesh's democratic backsliding demonstrates the limitations of engagement that prioritizes stability over democratic substance.

  

Youth engagement initiatives, civic education programs, and intergenerational mentorship structures provide long-term strategies for transmitting democratic values even in hostile legal environments. These approaches recognize that democratic transitions often span generations, requiring mechanisms for preserving democratic knowledge and commitments through extended periods of contestation. The 2018 student protests against Bangladesh's quota system in government employment demonstrated the democratic potential of youth mobilization, though sustainable change requires transforming such moments into ongoing processes of democratic renewal.

  

Critical junctures analysis offers a valuable methodological approach for identifying moments when authoritarian legal systems become vulnerable to democratic reform. By systematically analyzing conditions under which previous democratic breakthroughs occurred, reform advocates can strategically prepare for similar opportunities in the future. This approach acknowledges that democratic progress often occurs unevenly, with periods of apparent stagnation punctuated by moments of significant possibility. Strategic preparation for these moments—through coalition-building, proposal development, and capacity strengthening—transforms abstract democratic aspirations into concrete reform possibilities.

  

The integration of digital tools with traditional democratic organizing presents particular promise for overcoming legal barriers to democracy. Digital platforms enable documentation of abuses, coordination of resistance efforts, and dissemination of alternative narratives even when physical organizing becomes restricted. However, digital approaches remain vulnerable to surveillance, censorship, and disruption, requiring development of technical safeguards and operational security practices to preserve democratic space online. Bangladesh's experience with Digital Security Act enforcement demonstrates how authoritarian regimes have adapted repressive capabilities to digital contexts, necessitating corresponding adaptations in democratic practice.

  

Breaking the cycle of pessimistic expectations represents a crucial challenge in democratic struggles, as Svolik (2013) suggests in his analysis of democratic trajectories. The JusticeCraft framework, with its emphasis on concrete action, principled resistance, and institutional rebuilding, offers a promising approach for restoring belief in democratic possibility—perhaps the most important precondition for democratic renewal.

  

The JusticeCraft approach ultimately recognizes that legal barriers to democracy can only be overcome through strategies that are as sophisticated and multidimensional as the barriers themselves. By integrating resistance against unjust laws, reclamation of democratic principles, and resilience against authoritarian backlash, the framework offers a comprehensive approach to the challenging but necessary work of democratic renewal in contexts where legal systems have become obstacles rather than pathways to justice.
